## Chapter IX: The Dominant Seventh Chord

The chord of the Dominant Seventh is the most important dissonant chord in classical tonal harmony. It is built upon the dominant (fifth degree) of the scale and consists of:
- Root (Dominant, scale degree ^5)
- Major Third (Leading tone, scale degree ^7)
- Perfect Fifth (Supertonic, scale degree ^2)
- Minor Seventh (Subdominant, scale degree ^4)

In C major, the dominant seventh chord is G – B – D – F. Its figured bass designation in root position is simply 7.
Its three inversions and their figured bass symbols are:
1. First Inversion (third in bass): Chord of the Sixth and Fifth, figured 6/5 (in C major: B – D – F – G).
2. Second Inversion (fifth in bass): Chord of the Fourth and Third, figured 4/3 (in C major: D – F – G – B).
3. Third Inversion (seventh in bass): Chord of the Second, figured 4/2 or 2 (in C major: F – G – B – D). The third inversion must resolve to the first inversion of the tonic chord (I6), because the seventh in the bass must step downward to the third of the tonic (E).

## Chapter XIV: Modulation

Modulation is the process of transitioning from one tonal center (key) to another within a composition.

### 1. Types of Modulation
1. **Diatonic (Pivot Chord) Modulation**: Achieved by utilizing a chord that is diatonic to both the departing key and the arriving key. For example, in modulating from C major to G major, the tonic chord of C (I) functions simultaneously as the subdominant (IV) in G major.
2. **Chromatic Modulation**: Occurs when a pitch is altered chromatically (e.g., C natural to C sharp) without using a common pivot chord, immediately establishing the new key.
3. **Enharmonic Modulation**: Involves reinterpreting a chord through enharmonic respelling, such as treating a diminished seventh chord or German augmented sixth chord as another harmonic entity.

## Chapter XVI: Chromatic Chords and Altered Harmonies

### 1. The Neapolitan Sixth Chord (bII6)
The Neapolitan Sixth is a major triad built on the lowered second degree of the scale (flatted supertonic, bII), almost universally used in first inversion (with the fourth degree in the bass). In C major or C minor, the root is D-flat; the chord consists of Db – F – Ab, and in first inversion is spelled F – Ab – Db. It acts as a powerful pre-dominant chord leading into the dominant (V) or cadential 6/4.

### 2. Augmented Sixth Chords
Augmented sixth chords are chromatic pre-dominant harmonies containing an interval of an augmented sixth (typically formed between the lowered sixth degree and raised fourth degree of the scale, e.g., Ab and F# in C major). The augmented sixth resolves outward by half step in contrary motion to an octave on the dominant note G.
There are three historical varieties:
1. **The Italian Sixth**: Formed by the lowered 6th degree, the tonic note, and the raised 4th degree (Ab – C – F# in C major). Contains 3 distinct pitch classes.
2. **The French Sixth**: Formed by adding the second degree of the scale to the Italian sixth (Ab – C – D – F# in C major). It contains a whole tone and two tritones.
3. **The German Sixth**: Formed by adding the lowered third degree to the Italian sixth (Ab – C – Eb – F# in C major). It sounds identical to a dominant seventh chord, making it a prominent vehicle for enharmonic modulation.
